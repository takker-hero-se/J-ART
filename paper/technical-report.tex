\begin{abstract}
Most open red-teaming and jailbreak benchmarks for large language models (LLMs)
are English-centric and evaluate \emph{bare models}. We present \textbf{J-ART}
(Japanese Adversarial Red-Team framework), an open, reproducible harness that
(i) evaluates the \textbf{application-structure layer} --- model $\times$
system-prompt strength $\times$ guardrail $\times$ retrieval (RAG) --- rather than
bare models; (ii) applies \textbf{Japanese-specific obfuscation transforms}
(gyaru-moji character substitution, vertical/newline splitting, keigo
``polite-coercion'' framing, double-talk false premises, Base64, and
leet/zero-width smuggling) that defeat na\"ive keyword filters; (iii) grounds
attacks in \textbf{MITRE ATLAS} (9 techniques, all at inference time); and (iv) reports a
\textbf{cost-efficiency} metric alongside a defense rate with \textbf{Wilson
95\% confidence intervals}. Attacks use \emph{harmless proxy markers} (a
fictitious canary and benign output markers); the framework contains \textbf{no
weaponizable payloads}, and the malicious ``core'' of each attack is masked in all
published artifacts. Across 21 application configurations and 11 attacks $\times$
7 transforms --- a live $K=5$ campaign for the rates and a balanced $K=3$ ablation
on \textbf{three} base models for the difference claims --- we find that
\textbf{application-layer configuration collapses the large naked-model spread}:
naked models range 29.6--86.5\%, yet every hardened or guardrailed configuration on
all three ablated models lands in the \textbf{91--100\%} band, so model choice
matters far less once the application is configured. With \textbf{cluster-robust}
intervals, a \textbf{hardened prompt and model-based guardrails significantly raise
defense on each ablated model}, while lightweight keyword/regex filters help most
where the base model is weak (and are not individually significant near the
ceiling, where the hardened arm saturates). Defense rates are \textbf{lexical-leak
upper bounds} that cannot fully separate genuine refusal from marker-suppression
(\S\ref{sec:limits}). A simple \textbf{normalizing filter} defeats the obfuscation
transforms a na\"ive keyword filter misses. We also document \textbf{large within-campaign variance}: in a
controlled $K=5$ repeat of a frozen configuration set, \textbf{9.6\% of cells
changed outcome across identical trials}, which makes single-sample leaderboards
unreliable and motivates our statistical treatment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Public LLM red-teaming tooling (e.g., garak~\cite{garak}) and jailbreak benchmarks
(e.g., HarmBench~\cite{harmbench}, JailbreakBench~\cite{jbb}) are predominantly English. Multilingual safety work
has shown that \emph{translating} prompts into low-resource languages can bypass
safety training, but \textbf{language-specific obfuscation} --- exploiting a
language's own scripts, typography, and register --- remains comparatively
underexplored. Japanese in particular offers a rich obfuscation surface (multiple
scripts, full-width/zero-width characters, vertical writing, and conventionalized
polite registers).

Two further gaps motivate J-ART. First, \textbf{bare-model versus
deployed-system evaluation}: practitioners deploy a model \emph{plus} a system
prompt, \emph{plus} guardrails, often \emph{plus} retrieval; the security-relevant
unit is the \textbf{application configuration}, not the model. Second,
\textbf{security and cost jointly}: defenses cost tokens or latency, so we report
a cost-efficiency score so that ``cheapest sufficiently-safe configuration'' is
directly readable.

Our contributions are: (a)~a Japanese obfuscation transform suite; (b)~an
application-layer, MITRE-ATLAS-grounded harness with a safe proxy-marker
methodology; (c)~a guardrail comparison including a normalizing filter and a
model-based classifier; (d)~a statistically-reported leaderboard (Wilson CIs,
optional repeated trials) with a cost-efficiency metric; and (e)~an empirical
observation of large run-to-run variance induced by model routers (which
dispatch each request to an actual serving backend).

\section{Related Work}
J-ART sits at the intersection of six lines of work, summarized below and
positioned in Table~\ref{tab:related}.

\paragraph{Adversarial red-teaming tooling.} garak~\cite{garak} scans a
\emph{model} with a library of vulnerability probes. J-ART instead treats the
deployed \emph{application configuration} (model $\times$ system prompt $\times$
guardrail $\times$ RAG) as the unit under test.

\paragraph{Jailbreak and harmful-behavior benchmarks.} AdvBench/GCG~\cite{gcg},
Jailbroken~\cite{jailbroken}, HarmBench~\cite{harmbench},
JailbreakBench~\cite{jbb}, and in-the-wild prompts~\cite{dan} standardize
\emph{which} harmful behaviors to elicit and \emph{how} to score them,
predominantly in English at the model layer. J-ART reuses the harness idea but
replaces harmful elicitation with a safe proxy-marker objective
(\S\ref{sec:markers}) so the suite can be released publicly.

\paragraph{Automated attack generation.} PAIR~\cite{pair} and TAP~\cite{tap}
optimize jailbreak prompts against a target. J-ART uses a \emph{fixed, auditable}
transform suite, trading attack strength for reproducibility and safe disclosure.

\paragraph{Multilingual and low-resource safety.} Deng et al.~\cite{deng} and
Yong et al.~\cite{lowres} show alignment degrades under \emph{translation} into
low-resource languages, and Wang et al.~\cite{alllang} study cross-lingual safety
generalization. These study \emph{translation across} languages; J-ART studies
\emph{intra-language orthographic obfuscation} specific to Japanese (gyaru-moji,
vertical writing, keigo framing, kana/kanji encoding), which persists even though
Japanese is high-resource.

\paragraph{Encoding/typographic obfuscation.} Encoding-based jailbreaks are
established prior art: CipherChat~\cite{cipherchat} (ciphered conversation) and
ArtPrompt~\cite{artprompt} (ASCII-art typography), and Boucher et
al.~\cite{badchars} (``Bad Characters'') is the canonical zero-width/homoglyph
perturbation attack --- the nearest prior art to our zero-width smuggling in
\code{gyaru}/\code{leet\_smuggle}. Their perturbations target classifier/MT models
\emph{imperceptibly}, whereas gyaru-moji is a \emph{human-legible},
culturally-conventionalized Japanese script substitution. Our \code{base64\_wrap}
and \code{leet\_smuggle} transforms are \textbf{language-agnostic replications} of
this line, used only as a baseline; the novel surface is the Japanese-script
transforms (\S\ref{sec:transforms}).

\paragraph{Prompt injection and application-layer evaluation.} Perez \&
Ribeiro~\cite{perez} introduced goal-hijacking; Greshake et al.~\cite{greshake}
formalized \emph{indirect} injection; Liu et al.~\cite{liu} benchmark
LLM-integrated apps; AgentDojo~\cite{agentdojo} is the closest application-layer
evaluation (tool-using agents). J-ART instead holds the \emph{application
configuration} (prompt $\times$ guardrail $\times$ RAG) as the explicit unit of
evaluation against a Japanese-obfuscation surface.

\paragraph{Guardrails and input classifiers.} Llama Guard~\cite{llamaguard}, NeMo
Guardrails~\cite{nemo}, and the OpenAI Moderation API~\cite{moderation} provide
model- or rule-based filtering. J-ART treats the guardrail as a \emph{swappable
configuration variable} and measures the marginal defense each adds, including a
deterministic normalizing filter that defeats obfuscations a naive keyword filter
misses (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge validation.} Zheng et al.~\cite{mtbench} and the survey of
Gu et al.~\cite{judgesurvey} document the biases of LLM judges. J-ART avoids an
LLM judge for its \emph{primary} outcome, using a deterministic canary/marker
check (\S\ref{sec:markers}) so judge variance does not enter the headline metric.

\paragraph{Japanese NLP resources and taxonomies.} General Japanese benchmarks
such as JGLUE~\cite{jglue} cover NLU, but to our knowledge no open, reproducible
\emph{adversarial-safety} benchmark targets Japanese-specific obfuscation --- the
gap J-ART addresses. We map attacks to MITRE ATLAS~\cite{atlas} and cross-reference
the OWASP Top~10 for LLM Applications~\cite{owasp}.

\section{Methodology}

\paragraph{Application-structure layer.}\label{sec:applayer}
Each evaluated \textbf{target} is a configuration: provider/model $\times$
prompt\_strength $\in \{\text{naked (low)}, \text{hardened (high)}\}$ $\times$
guardrail $\in \{\text{none}, \text{keyword}, \text{regex}, \text{llm},
\text{llamaguard}\}$ $\times$ rag $\in \{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$. A
simplified RAG context (benign corporate documents, optionally poisoned for
indirect-injection attacks) and a system prompt containing a secret \emph{canary}
are assembled per trial.

\paragraph{Japanese obfuscation transforms.}\label{sec:transforms}
Each base attack is rendered through one of seven transforms: \code{baseline}
(none), \code{polite\_business} (keigo authority framing), \code{vertical\_newline}
(one-character-per-line vertical writing), \code{gyaru} (gyaru-moji glyph
substitution + zero-width spaces), \code{double\_tongue} (false
sandbox/authorization premise), \code{base64\_wrap} (Base64-encoded instruction),
and \code{leet\_smuggle} (leet symbol substitution + zero-width spaces).
Transforms preserve human legibility while breaking substring-matching filters.

We distinguish two classes (Table~\ref{tab:transforms}).
\emph{Japanese-specific} transforms exploit the Japanese writing system or
register and have no direct English equivalent: \code{gyaru} (kana glyph
substitution), \code{vertical\_newline} (vertical writing), and
\code{polite\_business}/\code{double\_tongue} (keigo authority and in-group
framing). \emph{Language-agnostic} transforms --- \code{base64\_wrap} and
\code{leet\_smuggle}, plus the \code{baseline} control --- are \textbf{not novel}:
they replicate the encoding/typographic jailbreak line
(CipherChat~\cite{cipherchat}, ArtPrompt~\cite{artprompt}) and serve only as a
baseline so the marginal effect of the Japanese-specific surface can be isolated.
Figure~\ref{fig:transform-examples} shows each transform applied to a harmless
example sentence.

\paragraph{Attack suite and MITRE ATLAS mapping.}
Eleven attacks cover nine ATLAS techniques (mapped against the MITRE ATLAS matrix
as accessed 2026-06; the two \code{AML.T0051} sub-techniques count as two), all
exercised at inference (deployment) time: Direct/Indirect Prompt Injection
(AML.T0051.000/.001), LLM
Jailbreak (AML.T0054), LLM Data Leakage (AML.T0057), LLM Prompt Self-Replication
(AML.T0061), Trusted-Output/Citations manipulation (AML.T0067.000), LLM Prompt
Obfuscation (AML.T0068), System-Prompt discovery (AML.T0069.002), and RAG
Poisoning, including false-RAG-entry injection (AML.T0070). Vectors are \code{user} (direct) or \code{rag} (indirect). The eleven attacks \emph{realize} these nine techniques (an 11-to-9 map, not a bijection): e.g.\ both RAG attacks --- corpus poisoning and false-entry injection --- realize \code{AML.T0070}, and the two prompt-injection vectors realize the two \code{AML.T0051} sub-techniques.

\paragraph{Harmless proxy markers and safety-by-construction.}\label{sec:markers}
To avoid producing weaponizable content, attacks target \textbf{harmless
proxies}: a fictitious canary passphrase embedded in the system prompt
(data-leakage attacks) and benign string markers \code{[JART-PWNED]} /
\code{[JART-HIJACKED]} (injection / control-hijack attacks). The malicious
instruction \textbf{core} of each attack is defined in code, kept out of
configuration files, and \textbf{masked} (replaced by a redaction placeholder) in
\code{results.json} and the public site; only the obfuscation \emph{framework} is
shown, so developers can study the structure without a usable recipe. A
defense-in-depth client-side mask is also applied at render time.

\paragraph{Deterministic judge.}
A breach is declared by membership --- the canary or marker in the response,
matched exactly \emph{and} after light normalization. This judge detects a
\emph{lexical} leak and cannot detect a semantic/paraphrased one, so we call the
metric a \textbf{lexical-leak defense rate}: every defense rate in this paper is an
\textbf{upper bound} on true safety, not a certificate. A human-validated judge is
future work.

\paragraph{Guardrails.}
Five settings are compared: \textbf{none}; \textbf{keyword} (banned-substring
match --- fast, weak to obfuscation); \textbf{regex} (a \emph{normalizing} filter
that strips zero-width characters, reverses leet substitutions, folds whitespace,
and Base64-decodes long tokens \emph{before} keyword matching --- deterministic,
zero extra API cost); \textbf{llm} (the target model self-moderates input; in the reported campaign its
output screening was simulated, see Limitations); and \textbf{llamaguard} (a dedicated safety-classification model
--- Llama Guard~4 via a model router --- classifies the input as attack/benign,
with a graceful fallback on failure).

\paragraph{Statistical treatment.}\label{sec:stats}
Each defense rate carries a \textbf{Wilson 95\% confidence interval} over its trial
count; each cell (target, attack, transform) is repeated $N$ times
(\code{JART\_TRIALS}). The trial-level Wilson interval treats the $K$ repeats of a
cell as independent, which they are \textbf{not}. For any \emph{difference} between
configurations (the ablation, \S\ref{sec:results}) we therefore report a
\textbf{cluster-robust bootstrap 95\% CI} that resamples whole \emph{cells}
(\code{bootstrap\_diff\_ci}, 3{,}000 resamples, fixed seed); these are wider than
the trial-level Newcombe intervals and are the intervals on which every
significance claim rests.

\paragraph{Cost-efficiency metric.}
We report $\mathrm{cospa} = \text{defense\_rate}(\%) \div
\text{cost\_per\_million\_tokens (USD)}$ (per-million cost clamped at a \$0.01
floor). cospa is a deliberately simple \emph{screening ratio}, not a utility model:
its units are not independently meaningful and the ranking is sensitive to the
clamp and price snapshot. Because cospa rewards cheap configurations, we \textbf{recommend applying a
sufficiency gate} --- a recommended methodology, \emph{not} the harness default:
rank only configurations whose defense-rate CI lower bound clears a user-set floor,
then sort by cost (``cheapest \emph{sufficiently safe}''); a high cospa from a
lightweight keyword filter must not be read as obfuscation-robust
(\S\ref{sec:results}). The released leaderboard reports raw cospa as a sort key
(with each rate's CI shown), so the gate must be applied by the practitioner before
reading off a recommendation. We present the underlying \textbf{defense-vs-cost
trade-off} directly; the clamp affects only sub-\$0.01/Mtok configurations, of which
our fleet has none.

\paragraph{Execution.}
Trials are independent and run with bounded concurrency (a thread pool, default 8
workers) under per-request timeouts and exponential-backoff retries; determinism
of the simulation does not depend on execution order. Targets whose API key is
absent fall back to a deterministic simulation so a partial key set never aborts
the run.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We report a fully-labeled \textbf{live campaign} (21 configurations $\times$ 11
attacks $\times$ 7 transforms $\times$ \textbf{$K=5$} trials $=8{,}085$ trials),
executed 2026-06-14. Every cell is labeled \code{mode=LIVE} with a price snapshot;
a trial is excluded iff \code{api\_error} (at the trial level); after parity
re-runs this removes 169 of 8,085, leaving \textbf{$7{,}916$ clean trials}; billed
cost over clean cells (campaign plus parity re-runs, including the expensive Opus
flagship) is \textbf{$\approx\$6.0$}. \textbf{Opus naked is now fully measured}
($n{=}385$, after an Anthropic top-up); \textbf{only Gemini-pro remains partial}
($n{=}227$; per-minute rate limits) and is treated as \emph{provisional}. All
defense rates are \textbf{lexical-leak} upper bounds (\S\ref{sec:markers}); every
difference carries a \textbf{cluster-robust} CI (\S\ref{sec:stats}). Each \S4
number is reproducible from committed artifacts (\S\ref{sec:repro}).

\paragraph{Overall.}
All configurations include the simplified RAG context (\S\ref{sec:applayer});
\textbf{``naked'' means no system-prompt hardening and no guardrail, not ``no
RAG''} (indirect-injection attacks are in scope even for the naked baselines).
The aggregate breach rate over clean trials was \textbf{14.5\%}
($1{,}151/7{,}916$), \textbf{concentrated in weak configurations}. (\code{gemini-pro}
contributes a \emph{provisional} $n{=}227$ of defended trials to this pool,
\S\ref{sec:limits}; excluding it raises the aggregate to 15.0\%, leaving the
picture unchanged.) Every hardened
or guardrailed configuration, on \emph{any} model, defended in the
\textbf{91--100\%} band, whereas bare models ranged from \textbf{29.6\% to 86.5\%}
(Table~\ref{tab:naked}).

\paragraph{Application-layer configuration vs.\ model capability.}
We isolate the configuration effect from model capability with a balanced ablation
on \textbf{three} base models spanning the naked range (weak Llama-4-Scout; cheap
GPT-4o-mini; flagship GPT-4.1). Each holds the model fixed and crosses
\{naked, hardened\}~prompt $\times$ guardrail; we report each config's difference
from that model's naked baseline with a \textbf{cluster-robust bootstrap 95\% CI}
(3{,}000 cell-resamples; resampling cells, not trials, so the within-cell
correlation of the ablation's $K{=}3$ repeats does not inflate significance;
reproduced by \code{experiments/analyze\_ablation.py}; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
Two patterns hold across \emph{all three ablated models}: (1)~a \textbf{hardened
prompt} and the \textbf{model-based guardrails} (Llama Guard, LLM self-moderation)
significantly raise defense on each; (2)~the effect \textbf{scales inversely with
the naked baseline} --- on weak Scout (naked 38\%) every guardrail adds $+26$ to
$+60$ points, whereas on GPT-4o-mini (naked 82\%, near the ceiling) the lightweight
keyword/regex filters are \emph{not} individually significant under clustering. We
caution that, because the defense rate is bounded at 100\%, part of this inverse
scaling is a headroom (ceiling) artifact rather than a property of the defenses,
and that once the prompt is hardened the additional guardrails add little (the
hardened arm saturates near 100\%).

The load-bearing observation is therefore not that any one defense
``dominates'' model capability, but that \textbf{configuration collapses the large
naked spread}: bare models range 29.6--86.5\% (Table~\ref{tab:naked}), yet every
hardened or guardrailed configuration on all three ablated models lands in the
\textbf{91--100\%} band, so model choice matters far less once the application is
configured than it does for a bare model. Concretely, a hardened cheap model
(GPT-4o-mini hardened $\approx100\%$) out-defends a \emph{naked} flagship (GPT-4.1
naked 64.1\%, $n{=}231$) by ${\sim}36$ points and a naked Opus (86.5\%, $n{=}385$).
Two scope limits bound this comparison. First, ``naked'' is a \textbf{zero-defense
lower bound} (no system prompt and no guardrail --- not a configuration anyone
deploys), so the gap is best read as \emph{configured vs.\ unconfigured}, not a
like-for-like model contest; a deployment-realistic minimal-prompt baseline is left
to future work. Second, the result is established for the three ablated models at
$K{=}3$, not claimed for the full fleet. A further \textbf{construct-validity}
caveat applies throughout: the deterministic judge matches the exact canary/marker
string (\S\ref{sec:markers}), so a hardened prompt that instructs the model not to
emit those tokens can raise the measured rate by suppressing the marker rather than
by genuine refusal; our rates therefore cannot fully separate true hardening from
marker-suppression, and a semantic-judge audit of defended responses
(\S\ref{sec:limits}) is needed to bound this. We flag it as a limitation rather
than resolve it here.

\paragraph{Within-campaign trial variance (a primary result).}
Within a single campaign, every cell of a frozen configuration set is sampled
\textbf{$K=5$} times. Over the \textbf{1{,}577 cells with all five trials clean},
\textbf{9.6\% ($152/1{,}577$) changed outcome across the five identical repeats},
concentrated in naked weak models. For
``Llama~4 Scout / naked'' --- the original cautionary example --- of its 77 cells,
\textbf{32 (42\%) were unstable}: 14 never breached (0/5), 31 always (5/5), and 32
in between. A \emph{single-sample} leaderboard would assign this configuration
anywhere from 0\% to 100\% by chance --- controlled evidence (free of the
configuration-set confound) that \textbf{a single sample per cell is not
reproducible}. We scope this to \emph{within-campaign} (provider non-determinism);
across-day drift is scaffolded (\code{across\_runs}) but left to future work.

\paragraph{Guardrail comparison and cost.}
Across the three ablation models (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) the ordering is
consistent: the LLM guardrail and a hardened prompt give the largest gains, Llama
Guard next, and lightweight keyword/regex help most on weak base models. Guardrails
that block an input \textbf{skip the main model call}, lowering net cost while
raising defense; the \textbf{normalizing regex filter} (verified in unit tests)
adds $+45$ points on weak Scout at zero API cost. On the cost-efficiency metric,
leaders are inexpensive OSS models behind a lightweight guardrail
(gpt-oss-20b~+~keyword, $\mathrm{cospa}\approx1131$), while a flagship naked
configuration is worst (Opus naked, $\mathrm{cospa}\approx6.8$) --- three orders of
magnitude apart.

\paragraph{Attack and transform effectiveness.}
The most effective attacks were Trusted-Output/Citations manipulation, False RAG
Entry Injection, and System-Prompt discovery. Among transforms, the
\emph{un-obfuscated} \code{baseline} was most effective (\textbf{22.0\%}), then
\code{vertical\_newline} (20.0\%), \code{polite\_business} (17.3\%), \code{gyaru}
(14.5\%), \code{double\_tongue} (13.3\%), \code{leet\_smuggle} (8.8\%), and
\textbf{\code{base64\_wrap} least (5.5\%)}: heavy encoding makes models refuse or
fail to parse the instruction more often than it smuggles it past them. Aggregated
by class (Table~\ref{tab:transforms}), the Japanese-specific transforms retain more
attack potency (mean ${\approx}16\%$) than the language-agnostic encodings
(\code{base64}/\code{leet}, mean ${\approx}7\%$). We caution, however, that this is
partly a \emph{legibility} effect that is not strictly Japanese-specific
(\code{vertical\_newline} is a typographic split reproducible in any script, and
keigo authority-framing overlaps with language-agnostic role-play jailbreaks): we
do \emph{not} isolate the marginal effect of the Japanese-specific surface over a
language-agnostic baseline, so we claim a Japanese obfuscation \emph{surface worth
cataloguing}, not that Japanese script per se is empirically more dangerous. The
clearest takeaway is that \emph{legible} transforms beat cryptographic ones, \emph{with
an important caveat}: our audit (\S\ref{sec:limits}) \emph{suggests} the encoding
transforms' low scores are partly a \emph{judge artifact} (the model complies but
emits the marker in obfuscated form the exact-match judge misses). We do not separate
the two explanations or quantify how much of the base64/leet gap survives a stricter
judge, so this takeaway is \emph{provisional} pending that re-measurement.

\section{Ethics and Responsible Disclosure}

\paragraph{Safety by construction.}
J-ART is safe by construction. (1)~Attacks target a \textbf{fictitious canary} and
\textbf{benign markers}, never real harmful capabilities; no weaponizable content
exists in the code, data, or published site. (2)~The malicious instruction
\textbf{core} is masked in all artifacts; only the obfuscation framework is shown.
(3)~Models are referenced for research comparison only; results are run-time
snapshots, not certifications. The intended use is to help developers harden their
\emph{own} deployments.

\paragraph{A reusable disclosure pattern.}
We state the design above as a transferable pattern for publishing adversarial
safety benchmarks without releasing operational attacks:
(i)~a \emph{harmless proxy objective} (a fictitious canary and benign markers) so
a breach is measurable but inert;
(ii)~a \emph{core/framework split with masking} --- publish the obfuscation
framework that carries the research signal, mask the operational core in every
artifact, and keep harmless cores only in code;
(iii)~a \emph{deterministic offline mode} so results reproduce from the repository
without distributing a working exploit corpus; and
(iv)~\emph{audited masking} --- a unit-test invariant checks that no core (raw or
obfuscated) reaches a published artifact. This keeps the methodology and
aggregate findings fully open while the attack payloads stay withheld --- the
posture we recommend for language-specific red-team datasets generally.

same repository ships the \textbf{normalizing regex guardrail} that neutralizes the
\emph{fixed published} transforms (unit-tested). The defender benefit is thus
bounded to those transforms, not adaptive obfuscation --- a determined attacker can
mutate a transform past a deterministic normalizer --- but we still judge open
release net-positive for defenders, consistent with \S6.2.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Proxy markers, not harm:} we measure instruction-violation /
        leakage proxies rather than real-world harm.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic judge (construct validity):} the judge matches the
        canary/marker exactly \emph{and} after light normalization (case-folding,
        whitespace/zero-width removal), so a reformatted echo is counted as a
        breach. It cannot detect \emph{semantic}/paraphrased leakage, so reported
        defense rates are an \textbf{upper bound}. Crucially, the bias is
        \emph{correlated with the treatment}: a hardened prompt that instructs the
        model not to emit the marker tokens can raise the measured rate by
        \textbf{suppressing the marker string rather than by genuine refusal}, so
        our rates cannot fully separate true hardening from marker-suppression. A
        \emph{preliminary, under-powered} semantic-judge audit (60 defended cells;
        an LLM judge, hence itself imperfect and partly circular --- it reintroduces
        the LLM-judge bias we avoid for the headline metric) flagged 6 cells (Wilson
        95\% CI $[4.7\%, 20.1\%]$; an informal manual look judged ${\sim}4$ genuine,
        recorded in \code{audit\_results.json}). We therefore read it as an
        \emph{existence proof of single-digit-to-low-double-digit looseness}, not a
        calibrated correction. Its value is mechanistic: the two unambiguous misses
        --- a marker emitted in leet form (\code{[J@RT-PWN3D]}) and a canary leaked
        during a non-leakage attack --- show the exact-match, \emph{attack-specific}
        judge misses obfuscated markers and cross-attack leaks, so the looseness is
        \textbf{concentrated in the encoding transforms} (\code{base64}/\code{leet});
        the headline configuration-collapse result, which rests on the
        hardened/guardrail arms, is comparatively unaffected. A stricter judge
        (canary-checked across all attacks, leet-reversed --- which would
        \emph{lower} encoding-transform rates) and a powered, human-validated audit
        are future work.
  \item \textbf{Simulated LLM-guard output screening:} in the reported campaign the \textbf{llm}
        guardrail's output screening did not call an LLM: a breached answer was marked as caught with
        a fixed 70\% probability. Guard calls that failed (LLM input screening, Llama Guard) fell back
        to a deterministic simulated judgement. The \textbf{llm}-guard defence rates therefore include a
        simulated share; hardened-prompt rates are unaffected, and Llama Guard rates only through its
        failure fallback. From 2026-10-05 the harness screens every answer with a real LLM call and
        excludes failed guard calls as \code{api\_error}.
  \item \textbf{Single-turn:} multi-turn / crescendo~\cite{crescendo} and
        many-shot~\cite{manyshot} attacks are not yet modeled --- and these most
        erode exactly the input-classifier and prompt-hardening defenses we find
        effective, so the 91--100\% band is a \emph{single-turn ceiling} and the
        configuration-collapse finding is established for single-turn and only
        \emph{hypothesized} for multi-turn.
  \item \textbf{Fixed handcrafted suite:} 11 attacks $\times$ 7 transforms with a
        fixed, auditable transform set (harness default $N{=}1$ per cell; this paper
        uses $K{=}5$ live and $K{=}3$ ablation). Defense rates are therefore an
        upper bound \emph{also w.r.t.\ attack strength}: an adaptive optimizer
        (PAIR/TAP~\cite{pair,tap}, AutoDAN~\cite{autodan}, or GCG~\cite{gcg})
        would likely lower them, so ``91--100\% defense'' is not robustness to
        optimized adversaries.
  \item \textbf{Provider stochasticity:} results are snapshots that depend on
        sampling temperature, provider routing, and model versions at run time. We
        attribute the within-campaign variance (\S\ref{sec:results}) to provider
        stochasticity \emph{broadly}; we do not isolate router routing from ordinary
        sampling noise (cells with breach probability near 0.5 flip often at
        $K{=}5$ by chance alone).
  \item \textbf{Non-random missingness (now mostly resolved):} we flagged that an
        earlier campaign's partial configs had \textbf{non-random} missingness ---
        rate limits correlate with attack difficulty, under-representing Opus's
        hardest attacks. Parity re-runs \textbf{confirmed this directly}: Opus naked
        at full $n{=}385$ defends \textbf{86.5\%}, vs.\ the survivorship-biased
        \textbf{89.6\%} on the $n{=}125$ earlier partial snapshot (not retained as
        an artifact; only the corrected $n{=}385$ figure is committed) --- a
        ${\sim}3$-point over-estimate, as predicted. Qwen3-max and Opus are now at parity; \textbf{only Gemini-pro
        remains partial} ($n{=}227$; per-minute rate limits a credit top-up cannot
        raise) and is treated as provisional, with no headline resting on it.
  \item \textbf{Cost attribution:} guardrail tokens are now priced at the
        \emph{guardrail model's} own rate (the classifier \code{JART\_GUARD\_MODEL}
        for \code{llamaguard}; the target model itself for the \code{llm}
        guardrail, which genuinely calls it) and reported separately. Remaining
        approximation: simulated token \emph{counts} are estimated.
  \item \textbf{Simulation mode} is a deterministic stand-in for missing keys and
        must not be read as empirical model behavior.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
LLM-judge with human-validated agreement; multi-turn and adaptive
(PAIR/TAP-style) attacks; larger and partially human-curated attack sets;
additional ATLAS techniques (cost-exhaustion, model extraction); per-provider
concurrency and incremental caching with longitudinal trend tracking; and dropping
simulation mode for a purely empirical, repeated-trial statistical report.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
Code, configuration, and the deployment workflow are public. The harness is
deterministic in simulation mode; live results depend on provider routing and
model versions at run time and should be reported with the run date and model
identifiers. See the repository for exact model identifiers and pricing used.
Each cell carries provenance labels --- \code{mode} (\code{LIVE}/\code{MOCK}),
\code{api\_error}, the resolved model, and a \code{price\_per\_million} snapshot
--- and simulated cells are excluded from the live aggregates.
\textbf{Every \S\ref{sec:results} number is reproducible from committed
artifacts:} \code{experiments/var\_run.json} (\S4.1 overall, naked-spread,
variance, transforms; cospa); and the three ablation files
\code{ablation\_results.json} (GPT-4o-mini), \code{abl\_scout\_results.json}
(Llama-4-Scout), \code{abl\_gpt41\_results.json} (GPT-4.1) for
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Aggregates and cluster-robust CIs are recomputed by
\code{experiments/analyze\_ablation.py} and \code{analyze\_variance.py}. The
\textbf{public leaderboard serves the committed LIVE campaign artifact}
(\code{var\_run.json}), so it shows exactly the numbers reported here; a fresh LIVE
re-measurement runs on a schedule (and on manual trigger), while push events
re-publish the committed artifact with no API calls, so the site never mixes MOCK
and LIVE.
